Chapter 8

Behaviors

Keys with more than one job: hold-taps, tap-dances, mod-morphs, sticky keys and macros.

In ZMK, everything a key can do is called a behavior. "Send a key" is a behavior; so is "switch layer". Those are built in, and you have been using them through the picker. Behaviors mode is where you build your own, with your own timing and your own combinations.

How Behaviors mode works

  1. Click Behaviors in the mode bar.
  2. On the left, under New, click the kind you want. It appears in the list and its settings open on the right.
  3. Give it a name you will recognize, and adjust its settings.
  4. Go back to Keyboard mode, select a key, and assign the behavior from the picker's Custom tab.

One behavior can be assigned to as many keys as you like. Change the behavior and every key that uses it changes with it.

Behaviors you define are built into ZMK firmware, and written to the keyboard on RMK for MoErgo and the Imprint's RMK fork, with their timing. Plain RMK on the Imprint and Dygma firmware cannot take them yet, so those boards have no Behaviors mode, and keys that use a behavior are flagged until the firmware changes.

Each behavior has two names. Name is for you. Label in the keymap is the short identifier written into the firmware files, such as hml; the app picks one for you and you rarely need to touch it.

A behavior from an imported layout can also have a Description, taken from the note written above it in the file. It is shown under the names and written back into the firmware files as a comment.

Where a behavior needs you to say what it does (what a tap sends, what three taps do), you see a small box showing a binding. Click the box to select it, then choose from the list beneath it, or type any ZMK binding and press Return.

To delete a behavior, select it and click Delete. The app refuses while any key still uses it, and tells you how many do; reassign those keys first.

Hold-tap

A hold-tap is the most useful behavior and the one that needs most care, because the keyboard has to guess: when you press the key, did you mean a tap or a hold? Guess wrong one way and you get stray letters; the other way and you get accidental shortcuts. The settings exist to make the guess match the way you type.

What it does

When held and When tapped each choose a kind of action:

ChoiceMeaning
KeySend a key or modifier.
Layer while heldTurn a layer on for as long as the key is down.
Go to layerSwitch to a layer and stay there.
Sticky layerTurn a layer on for the next key press.
Sticky keyHold a modifier for the next key press.

The hold-tap sets the kind of action. The particular key or layer is chosen on each key that uses it. That is why one "home-row mod" behavior can serve A-with-Control, S-with-Option and D-with-Command.

Deciding between hold and tap

The tapping term is the dividing line: release the key sooner and it is a tap; keep it down longer and it is a hold. The interesting case is when you press another key before the tapping term is up. The four flavors differ in what they do then:

FlavorWhat decidesFeels like
Hold-preferredHolds as soon as another key is pressed.Fast for shortcuts. Risky on letter keys: quick typing triggers holds.
BalancedHolds if another key is pressed and released while this one is down.The usual choice for home-row mods. Overlapping letters in fast typing stay as letters.
Tap-preferredHolds only after the tapping term, whatever else you press.Safest against accidental holds; you must wait out the term for every hold.
Tap-unless-interruptedTaps unless another key is pressed first.For a key you mostly tap and only hold together with others.

The timing settings

SettingWhat it doesAdvice
Tapping termHow long the key must be down to count as a hold on its own.200 ms suits thumb keys. Use 250 to 300 ms on letter keys.
Quick tapIf you tap the key and press it again within this time, the second press is always a tap, so holding it repeats the letter.Turn it on for letter keys. Without it you cannot hold the key to repeat.
Only hold after a pause in typingIf another key was pressed less than this long ago, the key is always a tap.The best cure for stray modifiers during fast typing. Try 150 ms.
Tap if held alone and releasedIf you hold past the tapping term and release without pressing anything else, send the tap anyway.Handy for slow, deliberate taps. Avoid on keys you hold while using the mouse.

Keys that can trigger the hold

A small keyboard drawing lets you limit which other keys can turn this key into a hold. Click keys to choose them, or use Any key, Left hand or Right hand. Pressing a key that is not chosen makes this key a tap immediately.

This is what makes home-row mods dependable. You hold Shift with one hand and press the letter with the other, so a left-hand mod should only hold for right-hand keys. Rolling two keys on the same hand then always types two letters. Decide on release delays that check until the modifier key is released, which you need when holding two modifiers on the same hand.

Firmware that knows which hand each key is on, RMK for MoErgo and the Imprint's RMK fork, offers a simpler switch instead: Hold only for a key on the other hand. One behavior then serves both hands, with no trigger keys to choose. On ZMK, which has no such switch, a layout that uses it is flagged; choose the other hand's keys as triggers instead.

Home-row mods

Home-row mods put Control, Option, Command and Shift under the fingers of your resting position: tap F for f, hold it for Shift. Your hands never leave the home row to reach a modifier.

They are famous for being fiddly to tune. The two buttons under Home-row mods create hold-taps that are already tuned sensibly:

  • Balanced flavor, tapping term 280 ms, quick tap 175 ms, hold only after a 150 ms pause in typing.
  • Left hand creates a behavior for keys on the left half that holds only for keys on the right half, and Right hand the reverse, both deciding on release.
Example: Shift on F and J
  1. In Behaviors mode, click Left hand and Right hand under Home-row mods. You get two behaviors, labeled hml and hmr.
  2. In Keyboard mode, on the base layer, select the F key. In the inspector's Binding box type &hml LSHFT F and press Return.
  3. Select the J key, type &hmr RSHFT J and press Return.

The first name after the behavior is the hold, the second the tap. Modifier names are LSHFT, LCTRL, LALT, LGUI and the same starting with R for the right-hand versions. Continue along the row for the other modifiers; a common order from little finger to index is Control, Option, Command, Shift.

Tuning by symptom
  • Stray capitals or shortcuts while typing fast: raise "Only hold after a pause in typing" (try 175 or 200 ms), or raise the tapping term.
  • Holds feel sluggish, or you get letters when you wanted a shortcut: lower the tapping term a little, or lower the pause.
  • Cannot repeat a letter by holding it: turn on quick tap; tap, then press and hold.

Tap-dance

A tap-dance counts how many times you tap the key in quick succession and does something different for each count. Under What each number of taps does there is one box per count; Add Tap adds another and Remove the last takes one away. Time allowed between taps is how long the keyboard waits for another tap before acting.

Example: Shift, or Caps Word on a double tap

Create a tap-dance. Set one tap to &sk LSHFT (a sticky Shift) and two taps to Caps word. Assign it to a thumb key. Tap once and the next letter is capitalized; tap twice and you type in capitals until the end of the word.

The cost of a tap-dance is a short delay: after a single tap the keyboard has to wait to see whether a second is coming. Keep them off keys you type constantly.

Mod-morph

A mod-morph sends one thing Normally and another With the modifiers held. Under Modifiers that switch it, choose which modifiers trigger the switch. By default the modifier is hidden from the second binding, so Shift+Backspace can send a plain Delete; choose modifiers under Modifiers passed on to the second binding to let them through.

Example: custom shifted punctuation

You want a key that types a comma, and a semicolon when shifted. Create a mod-morph: Normally , and with the modifiers held ;. Choose both Shifts as the modifiers that switch it. Assign it in place of your comma key.

Sticky key

A sticky key (also called a one-shot) holds a modifier for you until the next key press: tap Shift, release it, tap a, get A. The built-in sticky key is in ZMK's notation &sk LSHFT. Create your own when you want different timing:

  • Release after: how long the key stays sticky if you press nothing else.
  • Release as soon as the next key is pressed: lets you roll off quickly without capitalizing two letters.
  • Let modifiers through: pressing another modifier does not use up the sticky key, so you can tap Shift, then Control, then a key.

Macro

A macro plays back a sequence of steps when you press its key.

The quick way: type text

Type into the Type text box and press Return. The text is added as a series of taps, with Shift applied where needed.

The steps

StepWhat it does
TapPresses and releases a binding.
PressPresses and keeps it down, until a matching Release. Use this to hold a modifier across several taps.
ReleaseReleases something pressed earlier.
Change wait timeFrom this point, wait this long between steps.
Change tap timeFrom this point, hold each tap for this long.
Wait for key releasePauses until you let go of the macro's key. Steps before it happen on press; steps after it happen on release.
Pass parameterHands a value from the key's binding on to the next step, for macros that take parameters.

Under Timing, Wait between steps and Hold each tap for set the pace for the whole macro. If a program misses characters from a macro, slow it down here.

Example: a layer that holds a modifier

You want a key that, while held, switches to your Nav layer and holds Command, for switching apps with the arrow keys.

  1. Add a Press step and set it to &mo Nav. Add another Press for &kp LGUI.
  2. Add Wait for key release.
  3. Add two Release steps for the same two bindings.

Parameters a key passes to this macro (0, 1 or 2) is an advanced feature: it lets one macro be reused with a different key or layer on each key that uses it. Leave it at 0 unless you need that.

Do not put passwords in macros

A macro is stored unprotected in the keyboard and in your project file, and anyone who presses the key gets the text.

Chapter 9

Combos

Press two or more keys together to get something else.

A combo is a chord: press J and K at the same moment and the keyboard sends Escape instead of "jk". Combos give you extra keys without extra layers, and they work well for things you want close at hand but not on a key of their own: Escape, Tab, brackets, Caps Word.

Creating a combo

  1. Click Combos in the mode bar, then New Combo.
  2. Click the keys on the keyboard drawing that make up the combo. Click a key again to remove it. A combo needs at least two.
  3. Under Pressing them together does, click the binding box and choose what the combo sends.
  4. Give it a name so you can find it in the list later.

The drawing shows the layer selected in the sidebar, so you can see which letters you are combining. A combo is defined by key positions, not letters: if you later move J, the combo stays on the physical keys.

Combo settings

SettingWhat it doesAdvice
Keys must land withinHow close together the presses must be to count as a combo. The default is 50 ms.Lower it (30 to 40 ms) if the combo fires while typing normally. Raise it if you find it hard to trigger.
Only after a pause in typingThe combo only works if no other key was pressed in the moment before.The best protection for combos on letters you often roll, such as J and K.
Stay active until every key is releasedNormally the combo's key is released when you let go of the first key. With this on, it stays held until all are released.Turn it on for combos that act as a modifier or a layer key.
Active onThe layers the combo works on. With none chosen, it works on every layer.Limit a combo to your typing layers so it cannot fire in a game.
Example: J + K for Escape
  1. New Combo. Click the J and K keys.
  2. Set the binding to Esc. Name it "Escape".
  3. If "jk" words such as blackjack ever trigger it, lower Keys must land within to 35 ms.
Choosing good combos

Pick neighboring keys you can press with two fingers of one hand, and prefer pairs of letters that rarely follow each other in your language. Combos across the two halves work too.

ZMK limits how many combos one key can take part in and how many keys one combo can use. The limits are on the board's page, under Combos in its Settings tab; raise them there if you build a large set. Combos carry over to RMK and are written to the keyboard on RMK for MoErgo; Dygma firmware has no combos, so the mode is not offered on the Defy.

Chapter 10

Trackballs and touchpads

Pointing mode sets up what each pointing device on your keyboard does, and how fast the mouse keys move.

The Imprint can carry a trackball on each half, and the Go60 has a touchpad on each half. Pointing mode shows one card per device, followed by two cards for the mouse keys (see Mouse keys below). The Glove80 has no pointing device, so on it the mode has only the mouse-key cards; the Defy's firmware has no pointing settings and the mode is not offered.

The settings described here are ZMK's. On RMK for MoErgo and the Imprint's RMK fork, the touchpads' and trackballs' behavior is written to the keyboard with the layout; on plain RMK for the Imprint, pointing settings do not carry over yet and are flagged.

What the device does

ChoiceMeaning
Firmware defaultLeave the device as the keyboard's firmware sets it up. Nothing is written to your configuration for it.
Move the pointerThe device moves the mouse pointer.
ScrollThe device scrolls instead: roll or swipe up and down to scroll a page.

With two devices, a popular arrangement is one for the pointer and the other for scrolling.

Speed and direction

  • Speed offers seven presets from 25% to 300%. The − and + buttons beside them change the speed in steps of 5%. For scrolling, lower values (25% to 50%) are usually more comfortable.
  • A speed that is not one of the presets, such as one from an imported layout, is shown exactly, for example 92% (11/12).
  • Reverse left and right and Reverse up and down flip a direction. Reverse up and down on a scrolling device to switch between "natural" and traditional scrolling.
  • Swap the two axes exchanges horizontal and vertical, for a sensor that is mounted turned.

Right click

A click is a right click turns the device's own click, such as a tap on a touchpad, into a right click. It suits a device you use for scrolling, where a left click is rarely wanted.

A layer that switches on by itself

Layer to switch on while in use turns a layer on whenever you move the device and off again shortly after you stop. Stay on it for sets how long after the last movement, in steps of 100 ms.

Example: automatic mouse buttons
  1. Create a layer called Mouse. On it, assign LCLK and RCLK from the Mouse tab to two home-row keys. Leave everything else transparent.
  2. In Pointing mode, on the trackball's card, choose Move the pointer and select Mouse as the layer to switch on.
  3. Set Stay on it for to about 600 ms.

Now moving the ball turns your home-row keys into mouse buttons, and half a second after you stop they are letters again. If letters get swallowed just after you stop moving, shorten the time.

Different settings on particular layers

Each card ends with While particular layers are on. Under Add different settings for a layer, choose a layer to give the device its own speed, direction and click settings while that layer is on. The new settings start as a copy of the device's own, ready to adjust. Remove takes them away again.

While one of these layers is on, its settings are used in place of the device's own; they are not added to them.

Example: a slow layer for precise work
  1. Create a layer called Slow and put a key on your mouse layer that holds it.
  2. In Pointing mode, on the pointer device's card, choose Slow under Add different settings for a layer.
  3. In the new section, set Speed to 25%.

Holding the key now slows the pointer to a quarter of its speed for as long as you hold it.

Mouse keys

Keys that move the pointer or scroll (Chapter 7) have cards of their own: Mouse keys: pointer movement and Mouse keys: scrolling. They work on every keyboard, with or without a trackball or touchpad. Each has speed and direction settings, and can have different settings on particular layers in the same way as a device. A common use is a slow, a fast and a very fast layer for the mouse keys.

Devices the editor leaves alone

An imported layout can contain pointer processing the editor does not understand. The card then says so and leaves it as it is. Replace swaps that set-up for one you can edit here.

Chapter 11

Lighting

Lighting keys, lighting defaults, and painting a color onto every key of every layer.

Two kinds of lighting

Standard lighting is what ZMK does out of the box: all the LEDs show one color or one animated effect, and you control it with keys. This works with every firmware the app offers.

Per-key lighting gives each key its own color on each layer, so the lights show you what the layer does: arrows in green, the number pad in blue, the dangerous keys in red. ZMK does not include this. On ZMK it comes from community-made additions to the firmware and is experimental. RMK for MoErgo and the Imprint's RMK fork have it built in, with animations under the colors you do not set; Dygma firmware has it too, from a palette of sixteen colors.

Which of the two your board has depends on the firmware chosen on its page (Chapter 12). The Lighting mode appears in the editor only for a firmware with per-key lighting; colors you paint stay in the layout either way, out of sight until a firmware can show them.

Standard lighting: keys and defaults

The picker's Lighting tab has the keys. Put them on a system or function layer.

KeyWhat it does
RGB TOG, RGB ON, RGB OFFTurn the lighting on or off.
RGB BRI, RGB BRDBrighter, dimmer.
RGB HUI, RGB HUDStep the color around the color wheel.
RGB SAI, RGB SADMore vivid, more pastel.
RGB EFF, RGB EFRNext or previous effect: solid, breathe, spectrum, swirl.
RGB SPI, RGB SPDSpeed the effect up or slow it down.
RGB COLOR HSBJump to one specific color. Set hue (0 to 360), saturation and brightness with the inspector's Values steppers.

How the lights behave at power-up (on or off, starting color, starting effect, whether they switch off when idle) is under Lighting in the board's Settings tab; see Chapter 12.

Brightness limits protect the keyboard

LEDs draw far more power than the rest of the keyboard and warm the case. The app will not let brightness settings go above your keyboard's limit:

  • 40% on the Go60. MoErgo voids the warranty above this.
  • 80% on the Glove80. MoErgo's warranty limit; more draws current that can damage the computer.
  • 50% on the Imprint, Cyboard's own ceiling for its firmware.
  • The Defy has no published limit; Dygma's own app allows the full range.

On ZMK the limit is written into the firmware's settings. RMK for MoErgo and the Imprint's RMK fork keep the same limit inside the firmware itself, so the brightness setting on the board's page is a share of it. In Lighting mode the colors are drawn at full strength so you can tell them apart; on the keyboard they are dimmed to the limit. Lighting also shortens battery life considerably on wireless keyboards.

Per-key lighting

Switching it on

Per-key lighting is a property of the firmware, so it is chosen on the board's page: open the board's Firmware tab and pick a firmware that has it. The Lighting mode then appears in the editor. You can change back at any time; your colors are kept in the layout either way.

BoardFirmware with per-key lighting
ImprintZMK v0.3.0 with per-key lighting (experimental), built; or RMK for the Imprint (community fork, experimental), configured live.
Go60, Glove80MoErgo ZMK with per-key lighting (community, experimental), built; or RMK for MoErgo (community), configured live.
DefyDygma firmware, which has it as standard.
Experimental firmware

On the ZMK and RMK boards, per-key lighting relies on firmware that is not part of official ZMK and is not supported by MoErgo or Cyboard.

  • Go60 and Glove80 on ZMK: a community branch of MoErgo's ZMK, pinned to one commit. It is based on an older MoErgo release than the standard firmware, and ZMK Studio is not offered with it. After flashing, the colors appear only once you switch the lighting effect to the per-key one: press your RGB EFF key until your colors show. The keyboard remembers the choice. Make sure your layout has an RGB EFF key; the app warns you if it does not.
  • Imprint on ZMK: ZMK v0.3.0 with the community per-key lighting work applied, in a fork pinned to one commit. The order of the LEDs is taken from the wired Imprint and has not yet been confirmed on this keyboard, so colors may land on the wrong keys. The app warns you of this, and the LED check patterns below are for finding out.
  • Imprint on the RMK fork: the same unconfirmed LED order, and the firmware has not yet run on an Imprint at all.
  • Go60 and Glove80 on RMK for MoErgo: its authors describe it as experimental, and on the Go60 its README says the right half's LED order is an assumption.

If anything misbehaves, choose the standard firmware again on the board's page, build and flash. Chapter 15 has the way back for each keyboard.

Painting

  1. Select a layer in the sidebar. Each layer has its own colors.
  2. Choose a color: click a swatch, or use the color picker for any color.
  3. Click a key to paint it, or drag across keys to paint many. One drag is one undo step.

Colors are drawn at full strength so you can tell them apart. On the keyboard they are dimmed to the brightness limit.

What you can paint with

Paint withWhat the key's light does

Dygma firmware has no lock or battery lights; on the Defy, paint with plain colors. It also keeps a palette of sixteen colors for the whole keyboard, which the underglow shares, so a Defy layout that uses more different colors than fit is flagged before it is written.

Example: a battery gauge on four keys

On a system layer, paint four keys in a row with Battery above 20%, 40%, 60% and 80%, all in green. Switch to that layer and the row reads like a fuel gauge: four green is nearly full, two green and two red is around half.

LED check

Two more patterns, One color per row and One color per column, are for confirming that each LED sits under the key the app thinks it does. On a board whose LED order has not been seen on hardware, the Imprint, put one of them on the keyboard and look: a key lit in the wrong color is a key whose LED is mapped wrongly. Please report what you see, so the order can be corrected.

A good pattern

Give the base layer one calm color with Fill. On each other layer, paint only the keys that layer changes and leave the rest as "same as layer below". When you hold a layer key, exactly the keys that have changed light up.
